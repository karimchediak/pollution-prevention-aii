POLLUTION PREVENTION GUIDE

Pollution happens when harmful substances or waste enter the environment. Pollution can affect air, water, land, wildlife, ecosystems, and human health. Preventing pollution is usually more effective than cleaning it up after it has already caused damage.

Air pollution can come from vehicle exhaust, factories, power plants, burning fossil fuels, wildfires, construction dust, and burning trash. Common air pollutants can include tiny particles, ground-level ozone, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and carbon monoxide. Poor air quality can make breathing problems worse and can affect the heart and lungs.

People can help reduce air pollution by walking, biking, carpooling, using public transportation, avoiding unnecessary vehicle idling, saving electricity, and avoiding the burning of trash. Governments and companies can reduce air pollution by using cleaner energy, improving industrial filters, enforcing emissions standards, and supporting efficient transportation.

Water pollution occurs when harmful materials enter rivers, lakes, oceans, groundwater, or other water sources. Water pollution can be caused by sewage, oil spills, fertilizer runoff, pesticides, industrial chemicals, plastic waste, and litter. Polluted water can harm fish, wildlife, ecosystems, and people who depend on clean water.

Fertilizer runoff can carry nitrogen and phosphorus into waterways. Too many nutrients can cause algal blooms. Some algal blooms reduce oxygen in the water, making it difficult for fish and other aquatic organisms to survive. Farmers can reduce runoff by using fertilizers carefully, protecting soil, and maintaining vegetation near waterways.

Plastic pollution is caused when plastic products are discarded or escape waste systems. Plastic can enter oceans, rivers, parks, and neighborhoods. Large plastic items can harm animals through entanglement or ingestion. Over time, plastic can break into smaller pieces called microplastics.

Reducing single-use plastic can help prevent plastic pollution. People can use reusable water bottles, reusable bags, refillable containers, and reusable utensils. Communities can improve recycling systems, organize cleanups, and reduce unnecessary packaging. Properly disposing of trash also prevents waste from entering storm drains and waterways.

Waste prevention means reducing the amount of material that becomes waste. The waste-management hierarchy prioritizes reducing, reusing, recycling, recovering materials, and disposing of waste as a last option. Reducing waste often saves resources because fewer raw materials, less energy, and less transportation are needed.

Recycling turns some used materials into new products. Recycling can reduce the need to extract new resources, but it depends on clean sorting systems and local recycling rules. Not every item with a recycling symbol can be recycled in every community. People should check local guidance before placing materials in recycling bins.

Hazardous household waste can include paint, batteries, pesticides, cleaning chemicals, automotive fluids, and electronic waste. These materials should not be poured into drains, thrown into regular trash without checking local rules, or left where they can leak into soil or water. Many communities have special collection sites or events for hazardous waste.

Electronic waste, also called e-waste, includes discarded phones, computers, tablets, chargers, televisions, and batteries. E-waste can contain valuable materials, but it can also contain substances that should be handled safely. Donating, repairing, reusing, or using certified e-waste recycling programs can reduce environmental harm.

Environmental justice means that all communities should have fair protection from environmental hazards and fair access to environmental benefits. Pollution does not affect every community equally. Communities located near major roads, industrial sites, waste facilities, or power plants can experience higher exposure to pollution.

Pollution prevention can happen at individual, community, business, and government levels. Individuals can reduce waste and conserve resources. Businesses can improve manufacturing processes, reduce packaging, and prevent chemical leaks. Governments can enforce environmental standards, invest in clean infrastructure, monitor pollution, and support public education.

If someone sees an immediate environmental emergency, such as a major chemical spill, fire, dangerous fumes, or an oil release, they should move to a safe area and contact local emergency services or the appropriate local environmental authority. This educational app does not replace emergency professionals.
